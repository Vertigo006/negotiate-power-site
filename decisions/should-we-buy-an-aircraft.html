Should we buy an aircraft?

The right question is not whether ownership is attractive. It is whether ownership is the strongest path after mission demand, alternatives, economics, flexibility, and downside are tested together.

Discuss Your DecisionExplore BRENO

Direct answer

Aircraft ownership is supportable when the mission need is durable, the utilization case is real, control matters enough to justify the commitment, and credible access alternatives do not solve the problem more efficiently.

01

Mission demand

Separate recurring mission need from temporary peaks, convenience preferences, or demand that can be served through another access model.

02

Total economics

Compare acquisition, financing, fixed cost, variable cost, staffing, maintenance, management, downtime, resale exposure, and the cost of unused capacity.

03

Control and flexibility

Ask what ownership materially improves: dispatch control, cabin consistency, privacy, schedule reliability, mission fit, or operational flexibility.

What changes the direction?

Ownership should weaken when the assumptions carrying the case are fragile.

Watch utilization forecasts, peak-demand assumptions, residual-value expectations, staffing feasibility, hangar and maintenance constraints, financing terms, and whether the aircraft actually covers the mission set that justified the purchase.

Beyond the Brief: before buying capacity, test whether the actual constraint is access mix, scheduling, existing-fleet utilization, or mission segmentation.

Useful evidence

Do not start with the aircraft. Start with the missions.

Build the case from recent trip history, passenger loads, range, schedule patterns, peak periods, airports, dispatch requirements, current charter or fractional spend, missed or constrained missions, and the cost of alternatives. Then test candidate aircraft against that evidence.

For deeper background, see Aircraft Ownership vs. Charter and Aircraft Acquisition Decision Intelligence.
